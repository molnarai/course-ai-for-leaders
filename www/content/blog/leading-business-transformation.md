Most AI initiatives do not fail because the technology is weak. They stall because nobody agreed on what they were for, because the organization was not ready to absorb them, or because the team tried to jump from a demo straight to enterprise scale. This post sets out a three-step discipline for leading the change: **start with the goal, assess readiness along three pillars, and chart a path of nimble steps** to get there.
<!--more-->

## Why AI transformations stall

By 2025, generative AI was everywhere and its business impact was not. McKinsey's March 2025 *State of AI* survey found that only 21% of respondents reporting organizational generative-AI use said their organizations had fundamentally redesigned at least some workflows, even though workflow redesign was the attribute most strongly associated with reported EBIT impact among the 25 it examined. [mckinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/the%20state%20of%20ai/march%202025/the-state-of-ai-how-organizations-are-rewiring-to-capture-value_final.pdf) Adoption is also running ahead of preparation: OECD's November 2025 study of SMEs found that only 23.6% of those using generative AI reported employee participation in AI-related training. [oecd](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/generative-ai-and-the-sme-workforce_2d08b99d-en/full-report/component-6.html)

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has led a technology program:

- **Tool-first thinking.** Licenses are bought and access is rolled out, then someone asks what problem they solve.
- **The wrong diagnosis.** Leaders often assume employees are the main barrier. McKinsey's January 2025 *Superagency* research found employees were more ready to embrace AI than their leaders believed, and identified leadership direction and support as major constraints. [mckinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/superagency-in-the-workplace-empowering-people-to-unlock-ais-full-potential-at-work)
- **Pilot purgatory.** A proof of concept impresses in a demo but never becomes an operated, owned, funded business capability.
- **Big-bang plans.** A multi-year roadmap is approved on assumptions nobody has tested, and the first evidence that they were wrong arrives after most of the budget is spent.

Each of these is a leadership failure, not a technical one. The remedy is a simple sequence that leaders can own:

| Step | Leadership question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Start with the goal | What business outcome are we trying to change, and how will we know? | A use-case charter with a baseline, target, and intended scope |
| 2. Assess readiness | What stands between us and that outcome, across people, organization, and technology? | A readiness profile across three pillars, with evidence and critical gaps |
| 3. Chart the path | What is the smallest next step that closes a gap, creates value, and teaches us something? | A sequence of time-boxed steps, each with an owner, a measure, and a decision gate |

The rest of this post works through each step.

## Step 1: Start with the goal

"Adopt AI" is not a goal. Neither is "deploy a copilot to 5,000 employees." Those are means. A transformation goal names a **business outcome**, a **measure**, a **baseline**, and a **scope**.

| Weak goal | Strong goal |
|---|---|
| Use generative AI in customer service | Reduce average handling time on billing inquiries by 20% within two quarters, without lowering customer satisfaction or first-contact resolution |
| Give everyone an AI assistant | Cut the time to produce a first draft of standard client proposals from three days to one, with partner review unchanged |
| Become an AI-first company | Increase the share of underwriting decisions completed within 24 hours from 40% to 70% for small commercial policies |

### Write a use-case charter

Before any readiness assessment, write a one-page charter that answers:

1. **The problem.** What business problem exists today, and who experiences it?
2. **The baseline.** How does the process perform now, in measurable terms (time, cost, quality, error rate, customer outcome)?
3. **The target.** What improvement would matter, and by when?
4. **The alternatives.** Would process redesign, better rules, or conventional software solve this more cheaply? AI must earn its place.
5. **The intended scope.** Supervised pilot, departmental deployment, or enterprise operation? Readiness is always judged *for a scope*.
6. **The value mechanism.** How will an improvement turn into a business benefit?

The last question gets skipped most often. "Employees save two hours per week" is an intermediate result, not a benefit. Leadership must decide whether that time becomes more capacity, better service, less overtime, or another measurable gain. If nobody owns that decision, the savings disappear into the working week.

### Check that the goal is AI-suitable

Not every worthwhile goal is a good AI goal. A quick suitability screen keeps the portfolio honest:

| Criterion | High suitability | Medium suitability | Low suitability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business value | Clear, significant impact on KPIs or revenue
[pair](https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook/patterns) | Moderate impact on business outcomes | Limited or unclear business impact |
| Data availability | High-quality, relevant data or knowledge readily available [linkedin](https://www.linkedin.com/advice/3/how-do-you-measure-your-ai-systems-effectiveness) | Sufficient data, but requires preparation | Limited or poor-quality data |
| Problem fit | Language-, pattern-, or judgment-heavy work that rules handle poorly [mindtitan](https://mindtitan.com/resources/blog/criteria-for-success-in-ai/) | Partly solvable with rules | Easily solved with traditional methods |
| Technical feasibility | Existing AI tools can address the problem [authenticbrand](https://authenticbrand.com/guide/ai-readiness-what-business-leaders-need-to-know/) | Requires some customization | Requires significant research and development |
| Risk profile | Low risk, with clear guidelines for responsible use [weaver](https://weaver.com/resources/evaluating-ai-vendors-a-guide-for-selecting-genuine-ai-solutions/) | Manageable concerns with safeguards | High risk of harm, bias, or regulatory exposure |

AI suitability is not binary. [pecan](https://www.pecan.ai/blog/evaluating-ai-and-ml-solutions/) Use the screen to rank candidate goals and to avoid sending the organization after a problem AI will not solve.

## Step 2: Assess readiness along three pillars

With a clear goal, readiness has a specific meaning: **can we achieve this outcome, at this scope, with what we have today?** Three pillars structure the answer.

| Pillar | Core readiness question |
|---|---|
| **Workforce** | Can people use, evaluate, and work effectively with the AI solution? |
| **Organization and Leadership** | Can the organization direct, support, govern, and sustain the change? |
| **Technology and Systems** | Can the solution operate reliably, securely, and economically in its intended environment? |

The pillars are deliberately distinct. A workforce can be enthusiastic without organizational support. Leadership can be committed without an operationally viable system. Excellent technology can exist without a workforce or workflow able to use it. The assessment should show **which condition is actually limiting progress**, because that is where the next step should go.

This is a teaching framework, not a validated instrument. It draws on NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (governance, context, measurement, and lifecycle risk management) and on 2025 research linking AI value to leadership ownership, workforce development, workflow redesign, and infrastructure. [tsapps.nist](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=936225)

### Pillar 1: Workforce

Workforce readiness asks whether people can do the work the use case requires. It does not ask whether everyone can build AI models. OECD's 2025 research distinguishes the general AI literacy most workers need from the advanced skills that AI specialists need, and found that existing training leans toward the advanced end. [oecd](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/04/bridging-the-ai-skills-gap_b43c7c4a/66d0702e-en.pdf) Assess three distinct groups: **everyday users**, **workflow owners and managers**, and **technical specialists**.

| Subdimension | What to evaluate | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| AI literacy and appropriate reliance | Do employees understand what the system can and cannot do, including when not to rely on it? | Scenario-based assessments; explanations of limitations and prohibited uses |
| Role-specific proficiency | Can users, reviewers, managers, and specialists perform their respective tasks? | Demonstrations on representative work; role-specific competency checks |
| Critical evaluation and domain judgment | Can employees spot inaccurate, incomplete, biased, or unsuitable outputs? | Reviewed work samples; error-detection exercises; escalation decisions |
| Responsible use and data handling | Do employees know what information they may enter, which tools are approved, and how to report problems? | Practical policy scenarios; incident-reporting exercises |
| Willingness, trust, and participation | Do employees understand the change, help shape it, and feel able to raise concerns? | Interviews; anonymous surveys; evidence that feedback changed the implementation |
| Learning capacity and collaboration | Do employees have time, coaching, and access to business and technical expertise? | Protected learning time; coaching arrangements; cross-functional problem-solving |

Training attendance is not evidence of readiness. Look for **demonstrated performance on realistic tasks**.

### Pillar 2: Organization and Leadership

This pillar asks whether the organization can turn a promising application into a managed business capability. Whether leaders provide time, incentives, and resources belongs here, not under Workforce. Given the *Superagency* findings, it is often where the binding constraint sits.

| Subdimension | What to evaluate | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic alignment and business value | Is there a specific problem, a credible reason to use AI, and an outcome worth pursuing? | Use-case charter; baseline performance; comparison with non-AI alternatives |
| Executive sponsorship and accountability | Is a named leader accountable for results, with authority to remove barriers? | Sponsor mandate; decision rights; escalation process |
| Operating model and cross-functional ownership | Can business, IT, HR, security, legal, and risk make timely decisions together? | Named owners; responsibility matrix; delivery and review cadence |
| Workflow and role redesign | Have tasks, handoffs, human reviews, and exception handling been redesigned? | Current and proposed workflow maps; revised role descriptions |
| Change management and incentives | Are communication, employee involvement, manager support, and performance expectations aligned? | Change plan; consultation records; revised incentives |
| Governance and risk accountability | Are permitted uses, approval requirements, risk limits, and incident responsibilities clear? | AI policies; risk assessment; approval and exception records |
| Funding and value realization | Is there funding beyond the pilot, and a plan to turn improvements into business benefits? | Lifecycle budget; KPI ownership; benefits-realization plan |

McKinsey's 2025 findings associate stronger reported outcomes with workflow redesign, leadership commitment, and defined adoption practices. [mckinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) These are survey associations, not guarantees, but they point leaders at the levers they control.

### Pillar 3: Technology and Systems

Assess the whole operational solution, not just how the model performs in a demo.

| Subdimension | What to evaluate | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Data and knowledge readiness | Are sources accessible, reliable, current, permissioned, and suitable for the task? | Data inventory; access tests; quality and freshness checks |
| Task performance and evaluation | Does the solution meet defined quality and risk thresholds on representative cases? | Evaluation dataset; acceptance criteria; documented failure analysis |
| Integration and workflow fit | Can it connect to required systems while preserving permissions and business rules? | End-to-end tests; integration design; user acceptance results |
| Security, privacy, and access control | Are sensitive data, credentials, and system actions protected? | Security review; permission tests; privacy assessment |
| Reliability, scalability, and economics | Can it meet demand, response-time, availability, and cost requirements? | Load tests; service targets; cost per completed business task |
| Operational ownership and maintainability | Can the organization monitor, update, support, and recover it? | Named service owner; monitoring; runbooks; regression tests; rollback procedures |
| Human oversight and action controls | Can people review, intervene, or stop the system when needed? | Approval gates; action limits; fallback procedures; audit records |

For AI **agents**, the last row matters most. Generating a recommendation and executing a transaction need different controls.

### Rate each pillar on a common scale, for a stated scope

| Level | Definition | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Low** | Important capabilities or controls are missing, unclear, or untested. | Informal assurances, demos, or plans without verification |
| **2 — Developing** | Capabilities exist in a limited setting, but gaps remain before broader deployment. | Pilot results; partial training; documented but incompletely tested procedures |
| **3 — Ready for the intended scope** | Capabilities and controls are demonstrated under conditions representative of the proposed deployment. | Successful task assessments; approved ownership; tested safeguards; operational results |

"Ready" always needs a scope: ready for a supervised pilot, a departmental deployment, or enterprise operation. A team may be ready for an internal drafting assistant and not ready for autonomous customer-facing decisions.

### Keep two lenses across all three pillars

Rather than adding more pillars, apply two cross-cutting lenses to each one:

- **Business value.** Can employees produce better work? Can leadership capture the benefit? Can the technology deliver it at an acceptable total cost?
- **Responsible AI and risk.** Can employees recognize problems? Are leaders accountable for decisions? Do systems enforce safeguards and support monitoring?

This mirrors NIST's treatment of governance as a cross-cutting function rather than a one-time approval step. [tsapps.nist](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=936225)

### Do not let averages hide blockers

A readiness profile is a diagnostic, not a score to average. Some gaps should **block** a deployment at a given scope however strong everything else looks:

- Unresolved questions about lawful data access or data handling
- Output quality that has not been shown to meet the threshold for material decisions
- No accountable owner for the changed workflow or the running system
- No way for a human to review, intervene, or stop the system where needed

Blockers are not reasons to abandon the goal. They tell you where the first steps of the path must go.

## Step 3: Chart a path of nimble steps

The readiness profile shows the distance between today and the goal. The path closes it. Leaders are tempted to close it in one leap with a comprehensive program, a large budget, and a launch date. That bets everything on assumptions nobody has tested. The alternative is a sequence of **nimble steps**: each small, owned, and measured, and each one moves the organization forward while generating evidence for the next decision.

### What makes a step "nimble"

| Property | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Goal-anchored** | The step moves a measure in the use-case charter, or removes a named blocker to moving it. |
| **Gap-targeted** | It addresses the pillar that is currently the binding constraint, not the one that is most fun to work on. |
| **Small and time-boxed** | Weeks, not quarters. Small enough that failure is cheap and informative. |
| **Reversible** | It can be paused, rolled back, or redirected without stranding large investments. |
| **Owned** | One named person is accountable for the outcome, with the authority to act. |
| **Measured against a baseline** | Success criteria are set *before* the step starts: quality, rework, time, risk, and business outcome. |
| **Ends in a decision** | Every step ends at a gate: expand scope, adjust and repeat, or stop. |

### Move scope up a ladder, not all at once

The three readiness levels pair with a ladder of deployment scopes. Each rung needs readiness *for that rung*, not for the top of the ladder.

| Stage | Typical scope | What must be true to enter | What the stage must prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explore** | A few volunteers, sandbox, non-sensitive data | A charter and an approved tool | That the use case is plausible and worth measuring |
| **Controlled pilot** | One team, real work, every output reviewed by a human | No open blockers; baseline captured; users trained on the specific task | Measured improvement against baseline with acceptable quality and risk |
| **Defined-scope deployment** | A department or process, with routine (not universal) review | Redesigned workflow; named service owner; monitoring; funded run costs | That the benefit holds at volume and is captured in business results |
| **Scale** | Multiple units or enterprise-wide | Repeatable playbook; platform and governance capacity; value-realization tracking | That the capability can be replicated and sustained economically |

A "hub-and-spoke" operating model helps at the later stages. A central team supplies shared platforms, guardrails, and expertise; business units adapt them to local workflows. [datarobot](https://www.datarobot.com/blog/driving-ai-success-by-engaging-a-cross-functional-team/)

### Advance the three pillars together

Each stage should raise all three pillars enough to enter the next rung. A step that builds technology the workforce cannot use, or trains people for a workflow nobody has redesigned, creates the appearance of progress without the substance. A practical rule: **direct the next step at the lowest-rated pillar that blocks the next scope**, and keep the other two from falling behind.

### A worked example

*A regional insurer wants to reduce average handling time on billing inquiries by 20% within two quarters, without lowering customer satisfaction or first-contact resolution. The proposed solution is an AI assistant that drafts responses for service agents from policy and billing records.*

**Readiness profile, for the scope "controlled pilot":**

| Pillar | Rating | Key evidence and gaps |
|---|---|---|
| Workforce | 2 — Developing | Agents are eager and many already use consumer AI tools informally (a data-handling risk). No one has been assessed on spotting incorrect billing explanations. |
| Organization and Leadership | 1 — Low | The VP of Service supports the idea, but no one owns the changed workflow, the review step is undefined, and agents' performance metrics still reward speed alone. |
| Technology and Systems | 2 — Developing | A vendor demo performs well on generic questions. The billing system integration is untested, and there is no evaluation set of real inquiries. |

**Blockers:** No accountable workflow owner. Informal use of unapproved tools with customer data.

**The path:**

| # | Step (time box) | Pillar(s) | Owner | Success measure | Gate decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Name a workflow owner; issue interim guidance on approved tools and customer data (2 weeks) | Org & Leadership; Workforce | VP of Service | Owner appointed with decision rights; guidance acknowledged by all agents | Blockers cleared → proceed |
| 2 | Build an evaluation set of 200 real, anonymized billing inquiries with expert answers; test the assistant offline (3 weeks) | Technology | Service ops lead + IT | Accuracy and completeness meet thresholds agreed in advance | Meets threshold → pilot; otherwise adjust or stop |
| 3 | Map current and redesigned workflow with agents; define the human review step and escalation rules (2 weeks, in parallel with step 2) | Org & Leadership; Workforce | Workflow owner | Agreed workflow map; agents' concerns logged and answered | Workflow signed off |
| 4 | Train 12 pilot agents on the specific task, including error-detection exercises (1 week) | Workforce | Team manager | Agents pass a scenario-based check | Pass → begin pilot |
| 5 | Run a controlled pilot: one team, every draft reviewed, baseline comparison (6 weeks) | All three | Workflow owner | Handling time, CSAT, first-contact resolution, error rate, rework vs. baseline | Expand, adjust, or stop |
| 6 | Decide how to use the freed capacity; adjust performance metrics to reward quality as well as speed (2 weeks) | Org & Leadership | VP of Service | Benefits-realization plan approved | Prerequisite for the defined-scope stage |

None of these steps is dramatic, and each is cheap if it fails. Together they move all three pillars toward readiness for a pilot, and the pilot produces the evidence the leadership team needs to decide whether to expand. If step 2 shows the assistant cannot reliably explain billing adjustments, the organization has spent five weeks learning that, not a year.

### Measure value, not activity

"Number of users with access" and "prompts per week" measure activity. Each step should report against the charter: **quality, rework, cycle time, risk incidents, and the business outcome**, all compared to the baseline. Communicate results, including failures, regularly. That transparency builds trust in the program and keeps funding tied to evidence. [mckinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/the-organization-blog/four-critical-strategies-for-sustainable-gen-ai-adoption)

## What this asks of leaders

The framework is a discipline for leaders more than a tool for analysts. At each stage, a leader's job is different:

| Stage | The leader's job |
|---|---|
| **Goal** | Insist on a business outcome and a baseline. Say no to use cases that lack one. Decide in advance how gains will be captured. |
| **Readiness** | Ask for evidence, not assurances. Expect to find gaps in your own pillar (sponsorship, decision rights, incentives) and fix them first. |
| **Path** | Fund steps, not programs. Protect time for learning. Treat a well-run step that ends in "stop" as a success. Keep the gates honest. |
| **Throughout** | Involve the people whose work is changing; they hold the domain knowledge that makes redesign work. [weforum](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2024/05/5-strategies-to-make-the-generative-ai-transformation-a-business-success/) Be transparent about what AI can and cannot do. Model appropriate reliance yourself. |

Leading in this environment also means being comfortable with ambiguity: making decisions on partial evidence, balancing human judgment against AI-generated insight, and changing course when the evidence says to. [voltagecontrol](https://voltagecontrol.com/articles/adopting-ai-driven-change-management-key-strategies-for-organizational-growth/)

### Common traps

- **Starting from the tool.** If the charter was written after the purchase, revisit it honestly.
- **Blaming the workforce.** Check the Organization and Leadership pillar first. Unclear direction and missing support are common and fixable.
- **Mistaking usage for readiness.** Widespread use without training, guidelines, or review is a risk, not a capability.
- **Scoring by average.** A high overall score with an open blocker is still a "no" for that scope.
- **Skipping workflow redesign.** Putting AI on top of an unchanged process captures little value and often adds rework.
- **Leaving freed capacity unclaimed.** Without a value-realization plan, efficiency gains vanish.

## Applying the framework in this course

For your team project, apply the three steps to one specific use case and submit:

1. **A use-case charter:** the problem, baseline, target, alternatives considered, intended scope, and value mechanism.
2. **A readiness profile:** ratings (1–3) for each pillar and its subdimensions at the stated scope, with the evidence behind each rating and what is still unknown. Prefer task demonstrations, workflow maps, and pilot data over self-assessments.
3. **Your critical gaps:** the three highest-priority gaps, including any blockers, each with an owner and a corrective action.
4. **A path of nimble steps:** a sequence like the worked example, with time boxes, owners, success measures, and gate decisions.
5. **A recommendation:** explore, run a controlled pilot, deploy within a defined scope, or defer, and why.

A strong submission shows *which pillar is limiting progress* and makes the case that the first steps of the path are aimed there.
